## What was run

Five acquisition arms × 4 noise levels × 2 predictors, on the v2 stochastic pendulum datasets
(300k-rollout training pool; ground truth is a 158×315 grid with 90 rollouts per cell).

| arm | acquisition |
|---|---|
| `dir00` | non-adaptive, d2=0 — random prefix of the shuffled pool |
| `ent05` / `ent10` | label-entropy ranking, d2 = 0.5 / 1.0 |
| `tb05` / `tb10` | entropy + mode-separation tiebreak, d2 = 0.5 / 1.0 (FM only) |

d2 is the fraction of each epoch's budget the acquisition rule chooses; the rest is random.
Every arm acquires the same number of trajectories per epoch, so arms differ in *which* data
they get, never in how much. The classifier has no endpoint cloud, so the mode-separation
tiebreak is undefined for it and those two arms are FM-only.

## How claims are judged

Both sides of every comparison are Monte-Carlo estimates — the model's p from K samples, the
grid's p from M=90 rollouts — so a plain Brier score charges the model for noise it did not
create. Every squared-error quantity here is debiased on both sides; a perfect model scores 0.

There are two distinct noise sources, and conflating them is the main way to get a wrong
answer from this data:

1. **Eval-grid noise** — captured by the paired test. With ~39,770 cells the standard error is
   tiny, so this test declares almost anything significant. A |z| of 40 here means *these two
   runs* differ on the grid, not that the strategy works.
2. **Run-to-run noise** — the spread from retraining the same arm. Measured directly at
   epoch 0 (pre-acquisition, so all arms are the same configuration) and from seed replicates.

A gap only counts if it clears the *second* one. Several results that look overwhelming under
the paired test do not survive it, and the report labels them accordingly.

## Finding 1 — FM beats the classifier on calibration, not on ranking

Non-adaptive arm, equal data budget, deepest shared epoch:

| level | FM Brier_deb | CLF Brier_deb | FM skill | CLF skill | FM sAUROC | CLF sAUROC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| low | 0.00197 | 0.01002 | 0.991 | 0.956 | 0.9988 | 0.9992 |
| med | 0.00239 | 0.01669 | 0.989 | 0.924 | 0.9969 | 0.9973 |
| high | 0.00197 | 0.06941 | 0.990 | 0.638 | 0.9804 | 0.9804 |
| xhigh | 0.00240 | 0.07405 | 0.982 | 0.447 | 0.8959 | 0.8973 |

FM's debiased Brier is 5.1×–35× lower, and the classifier's skill score falls to 0.45 at
xhigh — barely better than predicting the base rate — while FM holds 0.982.

**But sAUROC is identical between them at every level, to 3–4 decimals.** The two models rank
states equally well; they differ only in how well the probability itself is calibrated. This is
why threshold metrics (F1, AUC, accuracy) looked healthy for the classifier throughout while
the probability metrics did not — those metrics only see the ranking.

## Finding 2 — entropy acquisition actively harms the classifier at high noise

Mean debiased Brier over the last 3 shared epochs, grouped by d2, with the per-epoch trend
(the least-squares slope of Brier against epoch — positive means the arm degrades as it acquires):

| level | d2=0 | d2=0.5 | d2=1.0 | slopes (d2=0 / 0.5 / 1.0) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| high | 0.0534 | 0.0827 | **0.1993** | −0.0004 / −0.0002 / **+0.0071** |
| xhigh | 0.0684 | 0.1044 | **0.1488** | −0.0004 / +0.0007 / **+0.0009** |
| low / med | — | — | — | no ordering |

(Both rows are the final window, epochs 16–18, with all 19 classifier epochs complete.)

Both the level and the *rate of degradation* increase monotonically with how much of the budget
the acquisition rule controls, sustained across many epochs at two independent noise levels.
That is much stronger evidence than any single-epoch gap: run-to-run noise has no reason to sort
itself by d2.

**Seed replicates (n=3 per arm) now settle it directly**, and reveal a crossover:

| level | epoch | non-adaptive (3 seeds) | fully-adaptive (3 seeds) | gap | 2×seed SD | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| med | 18 (final) | 0.01365 | 0.01127 | −0.00238 | 0.00590 | better, but **not** significant at the final epoch |
| high | **18** (final) | 0.05203 | 0.20947 | **+0.15744** | 0.03069 | adaptive **worse**, 5.1× the floor |
| xhigh | 15 | 0.07204 | 0.16016 | **+0.08812** | 0.01753 | adaptive **worse**, 5.0× the floor |

The **harm** at high and extreme noise is unambiguous and holds at the final epoch. The **benefit
at medium noise is not** — it fades with depth:

| epoch | med gap | 2×seed SD | significant |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | −0.00614 | 0.00362 | yes |
| 16 | −0.00792 | 0.00163 | yes |
| 17 | −0.00454 | 0.00087 | yes |
| 18 (final) | −0.00238 | 0.00590 | **no** |

The direction never changes — adaptive is ahead at every one of those epochs — but the gap
halves between epoch 16 and 18 while the non-adaptive seed spread triples (its three seeds land
at 0.00945 / 0.01394 / 0.01757). The defensible reading is that entropy acquisition gives the
classifier an early advantage at medium noise that the non-adaptive arm closes once it has
enough data, not a durable win. Flow matching at medium noise is the opposite: significant at
four consecutive epochs (7–10) and still holding.

All three now clear the run-to-run floor with n=3 seeds. At xhigh the seed distributions do not
even overlap — non-adaptive spans 0.0684–0.0743, fully-adaptive spans 0.1582–0.1751 — so the
separation is not a matter of averaging.

So the durable classifier result is the **harm** at high and extreme noise. The medium-noise
benefit is real in direction but transient in magnitude, and should not be presented as a win.

Note the two tests answer slightly different questions and agree where they overlap. The
dose-response asks whether all three d2 levels line up in order, and they do only at high and
xhigh. The replicate test compares d2=0 against d2=1.0 directly, and finds med significant too
— in the *opposite* direction. There is no contradiction: at med the middle arm (d2=0.5) sits
out of order, so no monotone trend is asserted, while the two extremes still differ. The
absence of any ordering at low is the control this whole claim needs — the analysis is not
manufacturing structure everywhere.

**Mechanism.** The acquisition score is the entropy of the *model's* p(success). Measuring the
true success probability of the states each arm actually acquired:

| level | frac ambiguous (random → scored) | mean true p (random → scored) |
|---|---|---|
| low | 0.027 → 0.296 (10× up) | 0.388 → 0.361 |
| med | 0.053 → 0.328 (6× up) | 0.389 → 0.179–0.367 |
| high | 0.133 → **0.010** (down) | 0.406 → **0.046** |
| xhigh | 0.269 → **0.080** (down) | 0.481 → **0.138** |

At low/med it finds genuinely ambiguous states and leaves the label balance intact. At
high/xhigh it *fails* — scoring worse than random on ambiguity — and instead harvests
overwhelmingly failure states, dragging the training distribution from ~41–48% success down to
~5–14% while the model is still scored on the whole space. Once heavy stochasticity
miscalibrates the model, maximum entropy in *model* space stops coinciding with genuine
ambiguity in *true* space, so acquisition selects a biased subpopulation, which worsens
calibration, which biases the next round.

**Selective prediction does not rescue it.** In the risk-coverage curves at xhigh, the
fully-adaptive arm has non-zero selective risk from ~5% coverage — it is wrong exactly where it
is most confident — whereas non-adaptive stays near zero out to ~30%. There is no confidence
threshold at which the adaptive classifier's predictions become reliable.

## Finding 3 — the same strategy helps flow matching and harms the classifier

FM never degrades the way the classifier does — no level produces a monotone-worsening ordering
in d2. At **medium** noise, with hardware and seed held fixed, FM is not merely unharmed but
clearly helped (n=3 per arm, all on the same GPU type):

| level | epochs | 2×SD test | ranges disjoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| med | 15, 16, 17 | significant at **all three** | **all three** |
| med | 18 (final) | not significant | **yes** |
| high | 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, 15, 18 | significant | **yes** |
| high | 10, 14, 16, 17 | not significant | 10: yes · 14, 16, 17: no |

Epoch 18 (the final epoch, complete as of 2026-08-07) is significant with disjoint ranges, but it
follows two consecutive failures at 16 and 17, so by the >=2-consecutive-epoch rule it does **not**
upgrade the verdict. Its mechanism is the same one: `ent10_s44` fell back to 0.00056, under the
non-adaptive minimum of 0.00096, and disjointness returned. Note also that the two tests disagree
here -- the 2xSD seed test calls epoch 18 significant while the epoch-0 noise-floor test in
`fm_vs_clf_divergence.png` calls the same gap n.s. When the denominators disagree, the weaker
reading is the one to quote.

Med's final epoch splits the two tests in the opposite direction: the gap *grows* (−0.00041 →
−0.00122) but the 2×SD test fails because the spread grows faster. The cause is a **baseline**
seed spiking — `dir00_s42` = 0.00318 against siblings at 0.00098 and 0.00057 — while all three
adaptive runs stay tightly grouped at 0.00029–0.00049 and every one of them beats every baseline
run. So the med effect is not weakening at depth; one non-adaptive run destabilised, which
inflates the SD in the direction that *flatters* the adaptive arm. The conservative reading is
that med is established by epochs 15–17 (three consecutive, both tests) and that epoch 18's
larger gap should not be quoted as the effect size.

**Both sets are now complete at the full 19 epochs** (all resumes finished 2026-08-07). The two
noise levels fail their deepest-epoch test for opposite reasons, and it is worth stating plainly
that neither failure is a weak effect: at high it is an *adaptive* seed spiking (16, 17), at med
a *baseline* seed spiking (18). With n=3, a single unstable run controls the variance estimate.
That is the campaign's main methodological limitation and it applies to every FM verdict here.

At high noise the aggregate test degrades after epoch 13, and the cause is entirely one run.
Per-seed debiased Brier:

| epoch | non-adaptive (3 seeds) | adaptive s42 | adaptive s43 | adaptive **s44** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 13 | 0.00093–0.00111 | 0.00022 | 0.00027 | 0.00027 |
| 14 | 0.00108–0.00243 | 0.00029 | 0.00035 | **0.00780** |
| 15 | 0.00110–0.00134 | 0.00023 | 0.00025 | 0.00030 |
| 16 | 0.00100–0.00127 | 0.00029 | 0.00031 | **0.00111** |
| 17 | 0.00100–0.00105 | 0.00033 | 0.00023 | **0.00147** |
| 18 | 0.00096–0.00106 | 0.00033 | 0.00022 | 0.00056 |

Two of the three adaptive runs hold a stable 3–4× advantage at **every** epoch, and all three
non-adaptive runs sit in a tight band. The third adaptive run destabilised at epoch 14 and never
recovered, and with n=3 its erratic values alone drive the mean and destroy the disjointness
test at epochs 14, 16 and 17. Instability is not specific to the adaptive arm — epoch 7's
outlier was a *non-adaptive* seed at 0.00704 — so this is occasional FM training instability at
depth rather than a bias.

The honest statement for high noise is therefore: **the benefit is large and consistent in five
of six runs, but the three-seed aggregate is not stable enough to certify it at the deepest
epochs.** Med has no such problem — all six runs are well behaved and it is significant with
disjoint ranges at 13–16. If this result matters for a paper, high noise needs more than three
seeds; that is the single clearest methodological gap left in this campaign.

`seed_variance.md` reports a non-parametric companion, `ranges disjoint?`, which asks whether
every seed of one arm beats every seed of the other — something one outlier cannot fake in the
favourable direction. For FM at high that is yes at every epoch measured (5–11); for FM at med, yes at 10–13; for the
classifier at high, yes at 15–18.

The defensible statement is therefore: **adaptive acquisition makes flow matching consistently
better at both medium and high noise** — every adaptive seed beats every non-adaptive seed at
every epoch measured — with the caveat that at n=3 the variance test occasionally fails when a
baseline seed spikes — epoch 7 (one non-adaptive run at 0.00704 vs siblings at
0.00140/0.00147) and epoch 10 (pooled SD 0.00297). In both cases the seed ranges remained
disjoint, so the effect was present and only the variance estimate was unstable. Nothing is detectable at xhigh, where FM's floor is very large. `seed_variance.md` now prints the verdict at every usable epoch, and
the contrast is stark:

| epoch | FM med | FM high | | epoch | CLF high |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 / 11 | no (−0.00085) | no (+0.00259 @ep1) | | 11 | **yes** (+0.13550) |
| 7 / 12 | **yes** (−0.00094) | **yes** (−0.00042 @ep2) | | 12 | **yes** (+0.14534) |
| 8 / 13 | **yes** (−0.00086) | no (−0.00032 @ep3) | | 13 | **yes** (+0.16028) |
| 9 / 14 | **yes** (−0.00130) | no (−0.00050 @ep4) | | 14 | **yes** (+0.14186) |

FM at high is significant in exactly one of four consecutive epochs and its gap even changes
sign — that is a fluke that happened to land on the deepest epoch, not a result. The classifier
at high is significant in all four with a consistent large gap. So: FM benefits at med,
is undetermined at high, and nothing is detectable at xhigh where its floor is very large.

So the headline is a **divergence, not an ablation**: entropy acquisition is the same procedure
in both cases, and at high noise it makes the generative model roughly 3× better while making
the discriminative one roughly 4× worse. The classifier side is settled by both tests at every
epoch 15–18; the FM side has disjoint seed ranges at epochs 5–7 but a flickering variance test,
so it is the weaker of the two halves. The mechanism explains the split. Acquisition shifts
the training pool toward failure states (mean true p from ~0.41 to ~0.05 at high). A classifier
fitted by cross-entropy inherits that marginal directly and its probabilities miscalibrate. Flow
matching models the endpoint *distribution* and reads p off MC samples, so a skewed marginal
costs it much less.

Caveat: FM's effects are large in relative terms but small in absolute terms (0.0009 of Brier at
med), well below the cross-hardware epoch-0 spread — real under a controlled comparison, and
invisible in an uncontrolled one. FM arms also reached only epochs 5–10 versus the classifier's
18, so FM has had far less budget to differentiate.

## Caveats

- **Depth.** FM arms reach epochs 4–7; the classifier reaches 13–17. FM is therefore compared
  over a much shorter horizon, and its "no effect" result is the weaker of the two claims:
  an effect could still emerge deeper. Comparisons are at matched epochs; the report prints
  per-arm depth.
- **Reproducibility.** FM training and its MC evaluation are not bit-reproducible, even on
  identical hardware, so a bit-matched control is unattainable. The epoch-0 spread is used as
  the run-to-run floor instead. The classifier *is* deterministic given a seed (epoch-0 spread
  exactly 0), so its floor comes from seed replicates.
- **Replicates.** n=3 seeds at med (both predictors) and at high/xhigh (classifier); FM
  replicates at high are still shallow (epoch 1), so FM's "no effect at high" leans on the
  epoch-0 floor rather than a replicate test. Everything else is n=1 per arm.
- **Two different floors.** The epoch-0 floor mixes GPU architectures and is therefore
  conservative; the seed-replicate floor holds hardware fixed and is much tighter (FM med:
  2×SD of 0.00305 vs 0.00028). A gap between those two thresholds — like FM at med — is real
  under a controlled comparison but would not survive a cross-hardware one.
- **The July FM baseline is not a valid control** — different hardware and months earlier. It
  is only used if no fresh control exists, and is labelled as unmatched wherever it appears.
